1 Introduction
Visual impairment represents a significant barrier to education and employment
opportunities worldwide. According to the World Health Organization, at least
2.2 billion people globally have a vision impairment, with a substantial portion
living in low- and middle-income countries. In Bangladesh, approximately 2.2
million people live with some form of visual impairment, yet less than one per-
cent of this population has access to formal Braille education. Traditional Braille
books are expensive to produce, bulky to store, and completely non-interactive,

2 Md A.H. Molla et al.
limiting their effectiveness as learning tools. Commercial refreshable Braille dis-
plays (RBDs) offer a dynamic alternative by using piezoelectric actuators to
raise and lower dots in real-time. However, these devices remain prohibitively
expensive for most institutions and individuals in developing countries, with
prices ranging from 2,000 to 5,000 USD [2]. A comprehensive survey by Etezad
et al. reviewed 96 papers across six actuator technologies and concluded that
high cost, bulkiness, and maintenance issues remain the primary barriers to
widespread adoption [2].
There exists a critical gap between the needs of visually impaired learners
in Bangladesh and the available assistive technology. An affordable, interactive
Braillelearningsystemmustprovidereliabletactilefeedbackthroughrefreshable
Braille dots that can change dynamically based on lesson content, support the
Bengali language with its unique Braille standard distinct from English [3], offer
mobility so that the robot can approach the user rather than requiring the user
to come to a fixed location [4], enable voice interactivity in Bangla for hands-free
operation [5], and be cost-effective with a total bill of materials under 100 USD
to ensure accessibility [6]. Recent studies have highlighted that factors such as
shortage of Braille resources, lack of teacher expertise, and insufficient policy
support further hinder Braille literacy in many regions [7].
The primary objectives of this research are to design and implement a six-dot
refreshable Braille cell using low-cost electromagnetic solenoids with soft tactile
feedback, to develop a comprehensive Bengali Braille mapping for all standard
consonants and vowels, to construct a four-wheel drive mobile platform capable
of basic autonomous navigation, to integrate Bangla voice control functional-
ity using natural language processing, and to achieve a total system cost below
10,000BangladeshiTaka(≈90USD).Asecondaryobjectiveistocreateanopen-
source design that can be reproduced and improved upon by other researchers
and makers, addressing the need for affordable Braille displays identified in prior
work [8]. The current prototype focuses on Bengali consonants (39 characters),
with demonstrated capability for expansion to vowels and compound charac-
ters. Audio processing, including Bangla speech recognition and text-to-speech,
is partially implemented but not fully integrated in the current version. The mo-
bility platform operates on flat, unobstructed surfaces, and obstacle detection
using ultrasonic sensors is reserved for future work. The system currently uses a
single Braille cell, which is sufficient for character-by-character instruction but
not for reading full words or sentences.
2 Literature Review and Related Works
2.1 Advances in Refreshable Braille Display Technologies
Refreshable Braille displays have evolved significantly over the past decade.
Etezad et al. conducted a comprehensive mapping review analyzing six actu-
ator technologies: piezoelectric, electromagnetic, electroactive polymers (EAP),
pneumatic, shape memory alloys (SMA), and microfluidic [2]. The review identi-
fied electromagnetic actuation as a promising low-cost alternative due to its fast

responsetimeandsimpleconstruction.Anelectromagneticbounce-typeactuator
demonstrated a 16 Hz refresh rate for single dots and high latching force without
constant power, thereby reducing energy consumption [1]. However, graphic re-
fresh rates remained slower at 2.7 Hz, highlighting trade-offs between speed and
complexity [1]. Alam et al. reviewed existing RBD technologies and proposed a
new actuation method to overcome the high costs that often exceed benefits for
visually impaired individuals [6]. The BrailleSense project achieved a 70% cost
reduction by using a rotating drum mechanism with magnetic encoders, though
mechanical wear remained a concern [9].
Soft robotics approaches have also been explored. Zhang et al. introduced
PneuGelSight, a vision-based proprioception system for soft robotic manipu-
lators [4], and electro-ribbon zipping actuators provided multi-directional hap-
tic feedback [10]. While promising, these methods often require high voltages
or bulky pneumatic systems, limiting portability [11]. Additional reviews have
summarized recent progress in flexible piezoelectric tactile sensors [12] and coop-
erative actuator systems based on dielectric elastomer transducers [13], both of
whichinformtheselectionofactuationtechnologiesforlow-costassistivedevices.

2.2 Bengali Language and Braille Standards
TheBengalilanguage,spokenbyover250millionpeopleprimarilyinBangladesh
andWestBengal,usesaBrahmic-derivedscript.TheofficialBengaliBraillestan-
dard maps each character to a unique six-dot pattern based on the international
Braille convention. Unlike English Braille, Bengali Braille maintains consistent

mapping across letters and numbers, simplifying initial learning. However, com-
plex conjunct characters (ligatures) pose challenges for automated translation.
Ahmed et al. developed a transformer-based neural machine translation system
specifically to address the conjunct character problem in Bangla Braille, achiev-
ing accurate translation of complex ligatures [3]. Kana and Hagos conducted a
systematic review identifying barriers to Braille instruction, including student
reliance on audio, lack of teacher expertise, and scarce Braille resources [7]. Their
findings underscore the need for culturally adapted, localized educational tools.
A scoping review on quality of life for Braille users by Rose et al. revealed that
existing research is fragmented and lacks consistent definitions of Braille literacy,
urging high-quality studies on long-term impact [14].
2.3 AI and NLP for Braille Education
RecentadvancesindeeplearninghavebeenappliedtoBrailleandtactilesensing.
Jeevakumari and Dey demonstrated improved visual speech recognition using a
3D-CNN with Bi-LSTM and Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) loss,
though performance was sensitive to lighting conditions [15]. Huang et al. used
instruction-tunedlargelanguagemodelstosolvecomplexBrailletaskslikemath-
ematics and mixed-language translation, but high computational requirements
limit mobile deployment [16]. For Bangla specifically, Hussain et al. developed
a deep learning-based voice-to-Braille conversion system using VGG-16 CNN
for phonetic audio recognition, achieving high accuracy in real-time conversion
of spoken Bangla to six-dot tactile patterns [5]. Souza Reis et al. created a
web-based pipeline using OpenAI Whisper and Gemini LLM that automated
lecture transcription to Braille files, though requiring stable internet connectiv-
ity [17]. Islam et al. developed a multilingual handwriting learning system for
visually impaired people using a stylus and graphics pad with voice guidance; a
Random Forest model achieved 99.8% accuracy in evaluating user progress [18].
Theirworkemphasizedtheimportanceofvoice-guidedinterfacesforindependent
learning. Additionally, recurrent neural networks and long short-term memory
networks have been comprehensively surveyed by Mienye et al. [19] and Krichen
and Mihoub [20] for applications in sequence modeling relevant to NLP tasks.

Mobilerobotictutorsforvisuallyimpairedindividualshavebeenexploredinedu-

have also been developed. Reddy et al. integrated Optical Character Recogni-

feedback [23]. However, the system relied on stable internet and was sensitive

2.5 Tactile Sensing and Haptic Feedback Mechanisms
Haptic feedback is critical for effective Braille reading. Shi and Shen summa-
rized state-of-the-art VR-related haptic sensing and feedback hardware, identi-
fying future prospects in AIoT and medical technology [26]. Ruan et al. reviewed
magnetic materials for haptic interfaces, noting that current response speeds and
miniaturization still lag real-time requirements [27]. Soft programmable buttons
with hydraulically coupled haptic feedback (HaptiCoil) developed by Youn et al.
provided wide-bandwidth tactile stimuli at low power levels under 40 mW [28].
FlatPanelHapticsbyShultzandHarrisonusedembeddedelectroosmoticpumps
for scalable shape displays, creating pop-up interfaces only 1.5 mm thick [29].
Jiang et al. conducted a systematic literature review on how haptic feedback
can assist people with blindness and low vision, providing design guidelines for
future assistive devices [30]. Their findings emphasize the importance of mul-
timodal feedback (tactile plus auditory) for effective information transfer. Ad-
ditional work includes a vision-based approach for sensorizing soft robots [4],
active electronic skin for ambient haptic feedback by Guo et al. [31], and a hy-
brid soft haptic display for rendering lump stiffness in remote palpation by Yu
et al. [32]. Recent advances in tactile sensory systems have been reviewed by Xi
et al. with focus on materials and fabrication techniques [33].

3 Methodology
The development of the BrailleBondhu tutor robot followed a five-phase iterative
methodology. First, requirements were gathered through interviews with special
education teachers in Dhaka and a review of existing literature [2,7] to identify
key functional and cost requirements. Second, components were selected based
oncost,availability,andperformance,includingsolenoidsevaluatedagainstprior
work [1] and NLP frameworks informed by recent studies [5]. Third, hardware
integration involved assembling the Braille cell, mobility platform, and control
electronics. Fourth, software was developed, including firmware for the ESP32
and a Python interface for the Raspberry Pi with Bangla speech recognition

The complete system architecture is illustrated in Figure 2. The system is
divided into two processing units: an ESP32 microcontroller for real-time con-
trol of actuators and motors, and a Raspberry Pi 4 for high-level tasks including
Bangla NLP and user interface management. Component selection was guided
by the following criteria. The ESP32 microcontroller was chosen for its low cost
(≈4.5 USD), 240 MHz dual-core processor, built-in Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, and
34 GPIO pins, sufficient for controlling six solenoids and four motors simulta-
neously. The Raspberry Pi 4 with 4 GB of memory was selected for its ability
to run a full Linux OS, support Python libraries for speech recognition (Vosk,
SpeechRecognition),andhandlefuturecomputervisiontasks[34].TheJF-0530B
solenoid is a 12 V push-pull device with 5 N force at 10 mm stroke; it was se-
lected after evaluating alternatives including servos and SMA wires for its cost-
to-performance ratio. The IRLZ34N MOSFET is a logic-level N-channel device
capable of switching up to 30 A, ensuring reliable solenoid actuation from the 3.3
V ESP32 output. The L298N motor driver is a dual H-bridge driver widely used
in educational robotics [21], capable of driving four DC motors with PWM speed
control. The INMP441 MEMS microphone provides an I2S digital interface with
high SNR (61 dBA), suitable for far-field voice capture, while the MAX98357A
amplifier drives a small speaker for text-to-speech output.
Fig.3: Power distribution schematic showing dual power bank configuration and
isolation between Braille and mobility subsystems.
Power is supplied by two separate 5 V power banks to provide electrical
isolation between the Braille actuation subsystem and the mobility subsystem,
preventing voltage drops caused by motor startup from affecting solenoid per-
formance [1]. The power distribution architecture is shown in Figure 3. The first
power bank (5 V, 3 A) powers the Raspberry Pi and, via an MT3608 boost
converter (5 V to 12 V), the six solenoids. A 1000µF capacitor smooths voltage
spikes, and a 120Ωdummy load prevents the power bank from entering sleep
mode. The second power bank (5 V, 2.4 A) powers the L298N motor driver via
a second MT3608 boost converter (5 V to 9 V). Common ground is established
between the two systems.

4 System Design
4.1 Hardware Architecture
The hardware architecture contains four fundamental subsystems which include
the Braille cell assembly and the mobility platform and the control electronics
and the audio subsystem. The Braille cell includes six JF-0530B solenoids which
engineers installed inside a 3D-printed PLA enclosure that measures 150 mm
by 100 mm by 60 mm. The lid contains two-by-three Braille grid holes which
direct each solenoid to match a 2.5 mm hole. A mechanical stop limits plunger
protrusion to 0.7 mm which matches the standard Braille dot height specified
in ISO 11548. The mobile chassis base contains an enclosure that secures the
enclosure with vibration-damping foam.

Figure4showshowhardwarecomponentsarephysicallyinstalledthroughout
the system. A commercially available four-wheel drive chassis kit (RBD-4017)
serves as the foundation of the mobility platform. The chassis dimensions reach
220 mm in length 140 mm in width and 60 mm in height while it contains four
BO geared motors with a nominal speed of 100 revolutions per minute at 6 V
which power 65 mm diameter wheels. The chassis supports the L298N motor
driver which gets installed at its front side while the ESP32 module sits at the
middle part of the device to achieve equal weight distribution. Two 400-point
breadboards serve as the base for assembling control electronics. Breadboard A
contains the ESP32, six MOSFET switching circuits (each with an IRLZ34N,
220Ωgate resistor, 10 kΩpull-down, and 1N4007 flyback diode), and terminal
blocks for solenoid connections. Breadboard B contains the L298N motor driver
with two MT3608 boost converters and power distribution terminals.
The Raspberry Pi audio system connects the INMP441 microphone through
I2S interface for superior audio recording. The MAX98357A amplifier drives a 3
W speaker for spoken feedback. The two components are installed on a compact
protoboard which connects to the 40-pin GPIO header of the Raspberry Pi. The

4.2 Software Architecture
ThesoftwarearchitectureexistsasalayereddesignwhichFigure5demonstrates.
The ESP32 firmware creates a command parser which operates on USB serial
at 115200 baud to process text commands that include BRAILLE:XXXXXX
and FORWARD and STOP. The Braille controller activates solenoids one at a
time with 30 millisecond intervals to decrease maximum power consumption.
The motor controller produces PWM signals which control the L298N motor
through speed and direction settings. The system reserves a sensor interface
space to support upcoming ultrasonic sensor installations.

5.2 Mobility Platform Performance
Mobility tests were conducted on a smooth tiled floor in a controlled indoor
environment with 20 trials per movement command. Forward movement over 1
m had a 95% success rate with an average deviation of 4.8 cm and an average
time of 3.2 s. Backward movement achieved 93% success with a deviation of 5.9
cm and a time of 3.5 s. Left turns of 90◦had a 90% success rate with an angular
error of±8◦, while right turns had an 88% success rate with an error of±10◦.
The asymmetry is attributed to minor differences in motor characteristics and
wheel traction; closed-loop feedback using wheel encoders could mitigate this in
future iterations [4].

5.3 Bangla Speech Recognition Accuracy
The Vosk-based Bangla speech recognition module was tested with 10 native
Bangla speakers (5 male, 5 female) each pronouncing the 39 Bengali consonants
in a quiet room with ambient noise of approximately 45 dB SPL. In quiet con-
ditions, accuracy was 94.2%. Under moderate noise of 60 dB SPL, accuracy
dropped to 87.5%, and under high noise of 75 dB SPL, accuracy fell to 72.3%.
Theseresultsarecomparabletocloud-basedsolutions[17]whilemaintaininguser
privacy and eliminating internet dependency. Performance degradation in noise
is consistent with findings from other voice-to-Braille systems [5]; future work
will incorporate noise-robust front-end processing such as spectral subtraction.

1 m forward, displayed a randomly selected Bengali consonant, and returned
to the starting position. The overall success rate was 90%, with Braille display
accuracy at 100% and mobility accuracy at 88% (failures due to wheel slip). The

6 Results and Analysis

within the acceptable range for interactive applications [1]. The dot protrusion
of 0.72 mm and latching force of 4.8 N ensure comfortable and recognizable tac-

navigation, with forward movement deviation under 5 cm per meter. The slight
asymmetry in turning accuracy can be addressed through closed-loop feedback.

7 Discussion and Conclusion
The results demonstrate that a functional, low-cost Braille tutor robot can
be built using off-the-shelf components available in developing countries. The
electromagnetic solenoid actuation proved reliable and cost-effective, validating
findings from prior research [1,2]. The integration of Bangla NLP addresses a
critical gap in assistive technology for the Bengali-speaking visually impaired
community, which has been underserved by commercial products [3,7]. The mo-
bility feature distinguishes this work from stationary Braille displays; the ability
to approach the user reduces cognitive and physical burden, which is particu-
larly beneficial for young learners and users with multiple disabilities [21]. The
open-source design aligns with calls for reproducible, affordable assistive tech-
nology [8].
However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The single-cell display
restricts the system to character-by-character instruction; multi-cell expansion is
necessary for reading words and sentences. The current mobility platform lacks
obstacle detection, limiting autonomous operation. Speech recognition accuracy
degrades in noisy environments, a common challenge for voice-based assistive
devices [5]. Despite these limitations, the project has successfully achieved its
primaryobjectives:alow-cost,voice-controlledrefreshableBrailletutorrobotfor
Bengali-speaking visually impaired individuals has been designed, implemented,
and evaluated. The total incremental cost of approximately 73 USD makes it
accessible to schools and individuals in Bangladesh, and the open-source design
contributes to the growing body of affordable assistive technology solutions for
underserved populations.

8 Future Works
Basedonthefindingsandlimitationsidentified,severaldirectionsforfuturework
are planned. First,multi-cell expansionwill develop a four-cell or eight-cell
Braille display using the same solenoid design, enabling word- and sentence-
level reading. This will require a multiplexing scheme to reduce GPIO count and
power consumption. Second,obstacle detection and autonomous naviga-
tionwill be implemented by integrating HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensors and a sim-
ple SLAM algorithm on the Raspberry Pi [4]. Third,enhanced Bangla NLP
will improve noise robustness of speech recognition using spectral subtraction
and beamforming techniques, and the vocabulary will be expanded to include
vowels, compound characters, and common Bangla words. Fourth,curriculum
integrationwill develop a structured, gamified Braille learning curriculum with
progressivedifficultylevelsdeliveredthroughaudiopromptsandtactilefeedback.
Fifth,battery managementwill incorporate an onboard system with Li-ion
cells, charging circuitry, and low-battery indication to replace external power
banks. Sixth,user studieswill conduct longitudinal evaluations with visually
impaired students in Bangladeshi schools to assess learning outcomes and user
satisfaction [14]. Finally,alternative soft actuation methodssuch as dielec-
tric elastomers [13] or electro-ribbon zipping [10] may be explored for quieter,
more skin-like tactile feedback.
